# The Middle Band Is Empty: A Two-Tier Divergence Guard, Measured — No Borderline Cases Pooled, All of Them Per-Scene

Author: Guangyi Zhao

## 1. Problem and background

The 2026-10-29 paper in this series closed with a judgment about its own guard: the divergence between two scale estimators is a graded risk signal, and pressing it into a binary gate makes the counts on either side of the gate absurdly sensitive to where the "bad estimate" line is drawn. The data suggested a concrete remedy. On the holdout set, the single-threshold guard (reject when divergence exceeds 5%) had false-rejected cases whose errors sat at +9.4%, +9.7% and +9.6% — within 0.6 points of the 10% "bad" line. A two-tier guard (a low line that only warns, a high line that rejects, and everything between labelled borderline) might catch exactly those coin-flip cases in a separate bucket instead of mixing them with real failures. This paper tests that remedy on a fresh holdout: how many borderline cases does a two-tier guard actually catch, and what does it cost end to end?

The answer runs against the guess. At deployment granularity (scenes pooled), the middle band is empty. Across the new holdout's 15 scales, the largest healthy-band divergence is 0.50% and the smallest failure-band divergence is 16.76%; between them lies a 16.26-point no-man's-land, and the pre-registered borderline band (2% to 15%) catches nothing at all. Pooled, the two-tier guard degenerates into the single-threshold guard scale by scale — same reject set, same deployed F1 at every scale, same number of U-route scales. Switch the granularity to per-scene, and the band comes alive: of 56 estimable per-scene decisions, 9 fall inside it, and their composition is exactly the mixture the design wanted to isolate — 6 near-line cases (error 5% to 10%), 1 genuinely bad case, 2 good cases under 5%. Borderline cases exist; they exist one level below the level at which deployment decisions are made. Whether a two-tier guard earns its keep depends on which level you act on.

The second result concerns a quantitative divergence-to-error mapping. A linear map frozen before the run from the 30 pooled points of the earlier papers (predicted error = max(0, -0.0108 + 0.9466 × |divergence|)) scores a mean absolute error of 0.0429 and a Spearman rank correlation of 0.8714 on the new pooled points: usable for ranking. But it is biased in two systematic ways — it predicts zero throughout the green zone, where a common-mode floor puts true errors at 1.38% to 3.91%, and it overestimates across the transition band, by up to 8.82 points. A risk score, not an error meter.

Setup is unchanged from the earlier papers: the Bloom-Counter colour-segmentation pipeline (baseline parameters min_area=120, bloom_area_min=900, kernel 7); the guarded estimator is BLIND7 (kernel 7, blind), the reference estimator BLIND3 (kernel 3); routing follows the estimate (at least 0.30 takes the P route, 0.15 to 0.30 the U route, below 0.15 the U route plus a move-the-camera alert). "Bad" still means an absolute estimation error above 10%, with a near-line band at 5% to 10% recorded separately. Baseline sanity F1 is 1.000.

## 2. Method

**Pre-registration.** Every threshold, the mapping coefficients and six predictions were frozen in data/2026-11-04-two-tier-guard-prereg.md before any new data was run, derived only from the two published datasets of 2026-10-29 (30 pooled points). Low line 2%: |divergence| at most 2% is GREEN, accepted (basis: the largest healthy-band divergence in both earlier pooled sets was 0.80%; the low line is 2.5 times that). High line 15%: divergence above 15% is RED, rejected (basis: the smallest failure-band divergence in the replication set was 17.42%, and the largest transition-band value in the old holdout, extremes excluded, was 13.65%; the high line sits between them). Between 2% and 15% is AMBER, borderline. The single-threshold comparator is the 2026-10-29 consistency guard unchanged: divergence above 5% rejects and falls back to U.

**Two-tier deployment actions (frozen).** GREEN deploys on BLIND7. AMBER takes RESCUE (route, and run P, with the BLIND3 estimate) and is entered in a borderline ledger. RED falls back to U. End-to-end cost is booked three ways: total F1 loss against the oracle, the number of scales whose deployment route changed, and the number of scales actually executed on the U route. The U-versus-P compute ratio is cited from the 2026-10-24 timing paper (roughly 5 to 33 times); this round counts scales and does not re-measure timing.

**Quantitative mapping (frozen).** The linear coefficients were least-squares fitted on the earlier 30 pooled points before this round (on that data: mean absolute error 0.0299, maximum deviation 0.0887). Frozen bins for |divergence|: 0–2%, 2–5%, 5–15%, 15–50%, above 50%, reporting per-bin error median and maximum. Spearman correlation is computed from ranks inside the bench script.

**New holdout.** Four scenes never used in any earlier paper (seeds 909, 1010, 1111, 1212; 209 bloom and bud targets in total), calibration references unchanged (3806.0 and 3740.0), the same 15 scales and pipeline parameters as 2026-10-29. The pooled 15 points are the deployment view; the nominal 60 per-scene points are a stress view — at s=0.07 all four scenes produce no BLIND7 estimate at all (clustering fails; by guard convention, no estimate means reject), so per-scene tier statistics use the 56 estimable points as denominator and the no-estimate cases are reported separately, never silently folded into a tier.

**Six frozen predictions.** P1: pooled GREEN is all good. P2: pooled RED is all bad. P3: pooled AMBER is non-empty and contains at least one bad or near-line case. P4: the frozen map's mean absolute error on the new pooled points is at most 0.05 and its Spearman correlation at least 0.80. P5: the two-tier total F1 loss is no worse than the single-threshold loss plus 0.005, and its U-fallback count is strictly smaller. P6: the per-scene AMBER share exceeds the pooled share.

Data and code: data/2026-11-04-two-tier-guard-bench.json; post-hoc summary data/2026-11-04-two-tier-guard-posthoc.json; pre-registration and bench script under the same date prefix.

## 3. Measurements

There are only two piles. All six GREEN scales have |divergence| at most 0.50%; all nine RED scales are at least 16.76%; the whole pre-registered borderline band sits inside the empty stretch between them. In bin terms: the 0–2% bin holds 6 points (error median 2.17%, maximum 3.91%, zero bad), the 2–5% and 5–15% bins hold zero points each, the 15–50% bin holds 8 (error median 11.79%, 7 bad), and one point sits above 50% (error 107.71%).

Applying the same two lines retroactively to the earlier sets (post-hoc, for comparison only) sharpens the point: the replication set is also GREEN 6, AMBER 0, RED 9 (healthy maximum 0.38%, failure minimum 17.42%). Only the old holdout is GREEN 6, AMBER 7, RED 2 — there, divergence climbed a ramp of 9.24% to 13.65%. Three datasets, one pair of lines, borderline occupancy 0, 7, 0. Occupancy is not a property of the guard; it is a property of how abruptly the guarded estimator fails on that scene set. This round's scenes fail off a cliff: one step from s=0.35 to s=0.30 flips BLIND7's error from -3.91% to +11.70% and divergence from -0.50% to +16.76%, with no transitional reading in between.

RED's composition deserves the same honesty as GREEN's: 8 bad cases and 1 near-line case. The near-line case is the familiar one — s=0.15, error +9.93%, 0.07 points short of the bad line. By definition it is not bad, and rejecting it books a false rejection; in substance it is the same creature as the +9.4% to +9.7% cases of 2026-10-29: continuous degradation flipping a coin at the definition line. It is also the entire reason P2 fails (Section 3.5).

### 3.2 Per-scene tiers: the borderline band lives at this granularity

Of the 56 estimable per-scene points (the four s=0.07 no-estimate points excluded and reported in Section 2): GREEN 24 (42.86%), AMBER 9 (16.07%), RED 23 (41.07%).

The nine AMBER points, individually: errors of 5.20%, 6.00%, 5.00%, 5.56%, 5.86%, 3.78%, 1.80%, 9.75% and 10.42% — six near-line, one bad, two good below 5%, median 5.56%. This is precisely the bucket the two-tier design was built for: under a single 5% line these nine would be split down the middle, half waved through as good and half rejected as bad, while in error terms they are one cluster of line-hugging cases. In bin terms they all sit in the 5–15% divergence bin (the per-scene 2–5% bin is empty too), with bin error median 5.56% and maximum 10.42%.

The other two tiers are comparably clean per scene: GREEN's 24 points contain zero bad cases (maximum error 6.11%, three near-line), and RED's 23 contain 22 bad and one near-line (error 8.60%). The mechanism is plain. Pooling clusters four scenes' contours together; the scenes' slightly staggered failure points average out and only the cliff remains. Per scene, each scene clusters its own fifty-odd contours; sampling jitter plus genuinely different per-scene degradation points mean that at a given scale some scenes have jumped and others are still at the edge, and transitional readings appear. The borderline band is not invented by the two-tier guard — it is the transition layer that finer granularity reveals, provided you actually make decisions, or at least keep books, at that granularity.

### 3.3 The quantitative map: ranking holds, readings are biased twice

Frozen map, new pooled points: mean absolute error 0.0429 (passes the pre-registered 0.05 line, without margin to spare), Spearman 0.8714 pooled and 0.8368 per scene. The shape of the deviations matters more than the average:

- All six green points are predicted as exactly 0 (the linear form goes negative and is clipped), while true errors run 1.38% to 3.91%. This is the common-mode floor: both estimators share the same contours and the same calibration reference; on new scenes the area median shifts as a whole and both estimators shift together (in the green zone their errors are both negative and never more than half a point apart). No divergence-only map can see this component. This round the green errors are all negative, where the old holdout's were +0.83% to +2.65% — even the sign of the common-mode component is a property of the scene set.
- All seven transition points (s=0.30 to 0.12) are overestimated: predictions of 14.79% to 19.57% against true errors of 9.93% to 13.32%, worst at s=0.12 (predicted 19.57%, true 10.75%, +8.82 points). Algebra makes this inevitable: BLIND7's error equals divergence plus BLIND3's error plus a cross term, and this round BLIND3 drifts further negative across the transition band (-4.33% to -9.08%) than it did in the fitting data, so the same divergence corresponds to a smaller BLIND7 error. A frozen map fitted on the average relationship systematically overestimates on scene sets where the reference estimator drifts harder.
- The two extreme points are comparatively accurate: s=0.10 predicted 31.74% against 37.50%, s=0.07 predicted 114.71% against 107.71%. Once divergence is large enough, the reference estimator's own error is a small perturbation.

So the map ends up with the same verdict the 2026-10-29 paper gave divergence itself, now with numbers attached: qualified as a ranking device (rank correlation 0.84 to 0.87), unqualified as a reading device. Any report that quotes its predicted error must quote the two bias bands with it — an invisible floor up to 3.91% in the green zone and overestimation up to 8.82 points in the transition band. Reporting only the mean absolute error of 0.0429 would average both biases away, which is its own kind of coin flip.

### 3.4 End to end: the two-tier guard equals the single-threshold guard, scale by scale

The deployment account is the bluntest table in this paper. Unguarded, the total F1 loss is 0.0122 (a single scale, s=0.28: F1 0.9854 against the oracle's 0.9976 — the same failure shape as in the earlier papers, BLIND7 talking a should-be-U scale into the P route). The two-tier and single-threshold deployments have identical F1 at all 15 scales and the same total loss of -0.0024 (at s=0.30 the guarded route beats the oracle by 0.0024, an artefact of the routing rule's own approximation, not a guard achievement — the convention was noted in the earlier papers). The route account is identical too: both guards change the deployment route at exactly two scales (s=0.30 and 0.28, P rerouted to U after rejection) and execute 9 scales on the U route, against 7 unguarded. The reason needs no searching: with pooled AMBER empty, the two-tier guard's middle action (RESCUE) is never triggered, its reject set is the single-threshold reject set scale for scale, and every downstream account necessarily matches. P5's F1 clause holds and its U-count clause fails — the pre-registration had guessed RESCUE would save a few U executions; with zero triggers there is nothing to save with.

The alert account also shows no separation this round, for the opposite reason to the earlier papers. At the three truly sub-0.15 scales (0.12, 0.10, 0.07), unguarded BLIND7 reads 0.1329, 0.1375 and 0.1454 — all below the alert line, so the unguarded alert is already 3 for 3. In the 2026-10-29 datasets those estimates climbed back above 0.15 (0.1809, 0.1834 and 0.1977, 0.1675) and only RESCUE restored the alerts. This round, even a +107.71% error at s=0.07 leaves the absolute reading below the line, because alerting tolerates estimation error multiplicatively. What a guard buys can differ completely between scene sets — the same lesson as tier occupancy, seen from another side.

### 3.5 Pre-registration scorecard

Six predictions. P1 (pooled GREEN all good): hit — six scales, maximum error 3.91%. P2 (pooled RED all bad): missed — 8 bad and 1 near-line, the near-line error +9.93%, 0.07 points from the definition line. P3 (pooled AMBER non-empty, containing a bad or near-line case): missed — pooled AMBER is 0; its per-scene counterpart holds (9 points, 1 bad and 6 near-line), but the pre-registration bet on the pooled granularity, bet on the wrong level, and the prediction is recorded as missed, not retroactively re-scoped. P4 (map MAE at most 0.05, Spearman at least 0.80): hit — 0.0429 and 0.8714. P5 (F1 clause plus strictly fewer U scales): half hit — the F1 clause holds (both guards at -0.0024), the U clause fails (9 against 9). P6 (per-scene AMBER share above pooled): hit — 16.07% against 0%. Total: three hits, one half, two misses. The three imperfect predictions (P2, P3, the back half of P5) all die on the same wrong assumption: I had taken the old holdout's gentle ramp as this pipeline's normal failure mode. The value of pre-registration is exactly this — the assumption was written down, so the post-mortem can point at the exact level where it broke instead of cherry-picking the two-tier guard's after-the-fact performance. The two-tier guard was not falsified this round; its conditions of applicability were measured, which is more useful than a vague pass.

## 4. Analysis and trade-offs

First: the value of a two-tier guard depends on the shape of the failure, so measure the shape before choosing the guard's form. Across the three pooled datasets in this series, the picture is now: replication — cliff, borderline occupancy 0; old holdout — ramp, occupancy 7; new holdout — cliff, occupancy 0. If your estimator fails off a cliff, a single threshold suffices and a second tier is a second pair of thresholds to maintain for no triggers. If it fails gradually, the borderline band separates line-hugging cases from the reject bucket and gives them their own ledger and their own action. Adopting two tiers before looking at a divergence histogram is paying maintenance for a middle band that may not exist. The measurement is cheap: bin historical divergence readings into the five frozen bins once — if the middle bins are empty, the two-tier guard has no work.

Second, my trade-off for this pipeline at its deployment granularity (pooled): keep the single 5% reject line — its pooled sensitivity is 1.000 on all three datasets, and this round its accuracy is 0.9333 with the single false rejection again a line-hugging case — and do not promote the two tiers to a deployment action. But keep AMBER as a per-scene bookkeeping tier: per-scene diagnostics are produced anyway for stability reporting, flagging 2–15% as borderline costs one field, and the nine points it catches (6 near-line, 1 bad, 2 good) are the only per-scene points a human needs to look at — GREEN is clean, RED is almost entirely bad, AMBER is where judgment lives. If deployment granularity ever moves to per-scene (single-camera independent calibration, say), the per-scene evidence for the two-tier form is already on the table: 16.07% occupancy, a near-line-dominated composition, and RESCUE's trigger set is exactly that bucket.

Third, the quantitative map's role is now settled: a risk-ranking tool. Its frozen coefficients (-0.0108, 0.9466) carry a rank correlation of 0.8714 into a third dataset, so the monotone divergence-to-error relationship is one of the more stable facts about this pipeline; but both reading-level biases reproduced with identifiable causes — an invisible common-mode floor (up to 3.91% here) and transition-band overestimation (up to 8.82 points) driven by the reference estimator's own drift. Quote the map's output only together with those two bands.

Fourth, a methodological note worth keeping: pre-registration turned a disappointing result into a located one. Three predictions failed on one shared assumption, and because the thresholds, the map and the predictions were frozen in writing, the failure can be assigned to a specific layer (pooled granularity, cliff-shaped failure) rather than absorbed into a narrative. A guard whose applicability conditions have been measured is worth more than a guard that vaguely passed.

## 5. Limitations and open problems

- One new scene set only. The cliff-versus-ramp distinction currently rests on three datasets (occupancy 0, 7, 0); what determines failure shape — scene composition, target counts, seed luck — has no controlled experiment here, and this paper draws no causal conclusion. That question has been added to the topic pool.
- All evidence still comes from one simulator family, new seeds from the same distribution — not a new camera and not real field imagery. The common-mode floor in real cross-scene deployment will be larger; this round's 3.91% is an illustrative lower bound.
- The AMBER deployment action (RESCUE) was triggered zero times at pooled level this round, so its end-to-end cost and benefit remain unmeasured in the pooled view. Per scene, only composition (error breakdown) was measured, not a per-scene RESCUE deployment F1 — per-scene deployment is not this pipeline's deployment form, and that account waits until the granularity actually changes.
- The map was fitted on 30 points with two parameters; the clipping and the negative intercept are small-sample roughness. This round tests the map and does not refit it; a revised map (for instance a bivariate one adding the reference estimator's own error term) is a new pre-registration, not a retroactive fix.
- The four per-scene no-estimate points (all scenes at s=0.07) are auto-rejected by convention and excluded from the tier denominators. Counted as RED, per-scene RED would be 27 and the AMBER share 15.00%; the conclusions are unchanged, and the convention is stated here rather than buried.
- Open problems, in queue order: the three-estimator median vote (bringing in BLINDRAW, which fails at the opposite end) is next; an independent reading to guard BLIND3 itself is still unanswered; and this round adds one — what decides whether the divergence distribution is a cliff or a ramp.
