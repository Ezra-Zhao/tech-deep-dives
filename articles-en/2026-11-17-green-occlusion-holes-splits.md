# The Leaf That Covers It Reports Nothing: A Heterochromatic-Occlusion Field Test — Buds Vanish Whole, Blooms Shatter Into Petal Islands

Author: Guangyi Zhao

## 1. The problem

In my 2026-11-07 test, the clutter and the true flowers belonged to the same colour family: a pink disc pressed against a flower merged with it in the mask, and the harm took the form of reclassification — a bud's merged area was pushed over the 900-pixel bloom threshold and it was relabelled a bloom, while not a single flower was ever truly lost (lost was 0 throughout). That article left one question open: what about heterochromatic clutter? A leaf — green, squarely outside the detector's petal colour ranges — pressed over a true flower adds nothing to the mask; it removes things. It digs holes, bites notches, and cuts contours in two. The expected victims are recall and area estimation, not classification.

This article measures exactly that (pre-registration in data/2026-11-17-green-occlusion-prereg.md, frozen before the main run, with predictions P1–P6). The occluder is a solid disc painted in the simulator's own foliage base colour, drawn last over a single true flower, sweeping disc radius (12/24/36) and centre distance (from separated, through tangent, down to concentric) — 312 trials in all.

The headline results first. Heterochromatic occlusion is the mirror image of same-colour merging almost everywhere. The same-colour harm curve was a valley (deepest overlap harmless, peak at a light touch); the occlusion curve is broadly monotone — the deeper the cover, the less area survives. Reclassification runs backwards too: same-colour clutter pushed buds up into blooms, occlusion bites blooms down below 900 and relabels them buds (buds are never reclassified; they simply disappear). The sharpest contrast is on buds: lost was 0 end-to-end in the same-colour test, while here a bud covered near its centre vanishes outright — with a radius-24 green disc, every bud at centre distance 15 or less was lost, 100%. And the green disc itself reports nothing: alone on foliage it produces zero contours and zero mask pixels, and none of the 47 end-to-end false positives came from the disc — every one was a shard of the host itself. One prediction I got wrong deserves the headline too: a radius-24 disc placed concentrically on a bloom was supposed to punch a hole the external contour would ignore. Instead it severed the petal bases, and the bloom fell apart into three to six petal islands, the largest holding a median 17% of the solo area, accompanied by an average of 3.5 false positives.

## 2. Method

- Base and hosts: single true flowers drawn fresh on the simulator's own foliage backgrounds (_foliage_background, seeds 7001–7006, six images), never saved as JPEG. Bloom r=38, bud r=15 — the same representative radii as the 2026-11-07 and 2026-11-16 tests. Host centres jittered within ±60 px of frame centre.
- Occluder: a solid disc in the simulator's leaf colour LEAF_BASE (BGR 45, 110, 55), drawn after the flower, on top. A pre-run assertion verified that a pure block of this colour yields zero mask pixels through the detector's _petal_mask (measured 0; the run only proceeded after the assertion passed). Radii rc = 12, 24, 36.
- Positions: disc centre along six rotating directions at centre distance dc from the host centre, from r+rc+10 (separated control) and r+rc (tangent) down to 0 (concentric), deduplicated per cell; bite depth bite = r+rc−dc recorded alongside. Six repetitions per condition, 312 trials total.
- Detection and four ledgers: detector defaults (min_area=120, bloom_area_min=900, kernel 7, CLOSE then OPEN), the series pipeline throughout. Each trial records four accounts: the fragment ledger (contours of area ≥120 intersecting the host disc count as host fragments — fragment count, largest-fragment area, and its ratio area_ratio to the solo reference); the centre ledger (the label of the contour containing the host centre versus truth, the correct/migrated/lost convention of 2026-11-07); the mask ledger (post-morphology petal-mask pixels inside the host disc, as mask_ratio against the solo reference); and the end-to-end ledger (the real detector, greedy centre matching against the single truth, TP/FP/FN).
- Solo references, measured in the same run: median bloom contour area 3864.0, median mask pixels 3920; bud 454.0 and 477. The green disc alone on foliage, three radii × six backgrounds = 18 trials: contours of area ≥120 always 0, mask pixels inside the disc always 0.
- Same-colour comparison numbers are quoted, not re-run, from the published 2026-11-07 article: r=12 same-colour disc merge rate 100% at gap=−8 down to 0% at gap ≥8, bud reclassification peak 14.3% at gap=−3, lost 0 throughout, and all 112 separated discs becoming false positives.

## 3. Measurements

### 3.1 Buds: cover the centre and the flower is simply gone

The bud's area ratio falls in one clean slope as the centre distance tightens. At rc=12: 0.968 at dc=21 (bite 6), 0.792 at dc=15, 0.523 at dc=12, 0.381 at dc=9 — and at dc=6 and dc=0 the largest fragment is exactly zero, a 100% loss rate. At rc=24, dc=27 already drops to 0.705 and dc=21 to 0.317 (loss rate 0.167), and all four cells at dc ≤15 are 100% lost. At rc=36, dc=39 still holds 0.683, and all five cells at dc ≤21 are wiped out. A bud's solo area is only 454, barely three times the 120 floor; a green disc biting from the rim toward the centre leaves a crescent that either falls under the floor or is eroded apart by the opening — there is no intermediate state to land in. The reclassification rate is 0 across the whole table: fragment area only moves downward, so a bud's label is either still bud, or there is no bud.

Set this beside the same-colour test: there, an r=12 disc pressed deepest onto a bud (gap=−18) left bud correctness at 100%. Same size, same position — a colour inside the mask is zero harm, a colour outside the mask is total loss. Whether occlusion hurts is decided first by whether the occluder's colour sits inside the detector's target ranges; geometry only gets a vote after that.

### 3.2 Blooms: first a notch, then petal islands

The bloom's area ratio also falls as the bite deepens, but in two regimes. The rim regime is a notch: at rc=24, area ratio 0.917/0.888/0.773/0.735 at dc=50/44/38/32 — the contour is still one flower with a bite taken out, label and count intact. The rc=36 notch runs deeper: 0.671 at dc=44, 0.517 at dc=32, still one flower.

Past the centre line the morphology changes. At rc=24, dc=12, area ratio is 0.479 and splitting appears (split rate 0.167); at dc=6 the split rate is 100% and area ratio 0.309; at dc=0 (concentric) the split rate is 0.833 and the largest fragment's median area ratio is just 0.170 — trial by trial, fragment counts of 1/6/5/3/3/5 with largest-fragment shares 0.52/0.07/0.13/0.35/0.20/0.14. The mechanism is plain once you look at how the simulator draws a bloom: six to eight elliptical petals arranged around a yellow centre disc, each petal touching the rest only near its base. A concentric radius-24 disc covers exactly the centre and every petal base; the surviving petal tips no longer touch each other and become separate islands. The rc=36 concentric case is total: mask ratio 0.0, loss rate 100%, the flower covered to the last pixel. At rc=36, dc=12, the largest fragment lands in [120, 900) in every trial and is reclassified as a bud (reclassification rate 1.000), with end-to-end FN 1.0.

The small rc=12 disc never harms a bloom: correctness 100% in every cell, worst area ratio 0.835 at dc=12. But it is non-monotone in one place: area ratio 0.835 at dc=12 recovers to 0.982/0.962 at dc=6/0. The cause is the island mechanism run in reverse: at dc=12 the disc sits on the petal ring and severs one or two petal bases, and a whole petal drops off; moved to the centre, the disc covers only the yellow centre and the inner petal bases, while the external contour — drawn through the petal tips — survives, and RETR_EXTERNAL computes area from that external contour, so the hole never reaches the area ledger. The mask ledger does see the hole (mask_ratio 0.852 at dc=0; the hole is real), the contour-area ledger does not.

### 3.3 Where the false positives come from: 47, all of them host shards

The green disc alone produces zero false positives (Section 2 references). Across the 312 main trials, end-to-end FP totals 47, all of them in five deep-bite bloom cells: FP means of 0.167/2.333/3.500 at rc=24, dc=12/6/0, and 1.667/0.167 at rc=36, dc=12/6. Trial-by-trial attribution finds exactly two sources. First, split fragments: once a bloom shatters into petal islands, at most one island can be claimed by centre matching (and only if its label agrees), and every remaining island is an FP. Second, reclassified remnants: a single surviving fragment whose area fell into [120, 900) is labelled bud, mismatches the bloom truth, and the same shard is booked as one FP plus one FN (two trials at rc=36 dc=12, one at dc=6). Contrast with the same-colour test, where separated clutter produced 112 FPs by impersonating flowers itself. Occlusion FPs are a true flower, cut into pieces, impersonating other flowers. The remedies point in opposite directions: same-colour FPs are governed by spacing and merging, occlusion FPs by fragment bookkeeping (merge same-neighbourhood shards before counting).

### 3.4 Two ledgers diverge: the centre account pronounces living buds dead

The outcome convention itself took a hit in this test. For buds at rc=12, dc=12 and dc=9, the centre ledger's loss rates are 0.833 and 1.000 — the disc covers the bud's centre, no contour contains the centre point, so by the centre convention the flower is dead. But the fragment ledger scores both cells 100% correct (the surviving crescent holds 0.52/0.38 of the solo area and is still labelled bud), end-to-end FN is 0, and the detector demonstrably reports the flower. In the other direction, blooms at rc=24, dc=0 have a centre-ledger loss rate of 100% yet a fragment correctness of 0.333 (one petal island still exceeds 900) and end-to-end FN of only 0.667. The centre ledger measures whether the centre point still sits inside a contour, and occlusion aims precisely at the centre, so it systematically overstates loss; the fragment and end-to-end ledgers are the ones that match what a deployment actually receives. Any evaluation that judges a flower's survival by a centre-point test — the contour method of the earlier articles in this series included — must be discounted under occlusion-type harm; from this article on, I report both ledgers side by side.

### 3.5 Prediction scorecard

- P1 (the green disc is itself invisible; FPs come only from split fragments): half. The disc alone is 0 in 18/18 trials and no FP in the main run traces to the disc itself — that half holds in full. But the FP sources include three single-fragment reclassified remnants (Section 3.3, second source), a category the pre-registration failed to enumerate.
- P2 (a concentric hole the external contour ignores): half. At rc=12 concentric, area ratio 0.962 (above the 0.90 line) and correctness 100% — holds; but mask_ratio is 0.852, not below the pre-registered 0.80 — the hole is smaller than I estimated. At rc=24 concentric the prediction fails outright: area ratio 0.170, not ≥0.90, and the morphology is not a hole but severed petal bases and islands (Section 3.2). At rc=36 concentric, loss is 100%, consistent with the "below 0.50 or lost" branch.
- P3 (area falls monotonically as the bite deepens): half. Five of the six (host, rc) curves are monotone within tolerance; the single exception is the bloom rc=12 curve's last step (0.835 at dc=12 rising to 0.982 at dc=6, +0.147), mechanised in Section 3.2: a small disc moving off the petal ring into the centre does less damage, not more. So the same-colour valley is not wholly overturned — a miniature version of it survives in the "small occluder on a multi-petal flower" corner. The main morphology is still a monotone downslope.
- P4 (reverse reclassification): full hit. Bloom reclassification appears in four cells (rc=24 dc=6/0, rc=36 dc=12/6, peaking at 1.000); bud reclassification is 0 everywhere.
- P5 (bud fragility): full hit, and more fragile than predicted. Every cell with rc ≥24 and dc ≤6 is 100% lost; the rc=12 concentric cell is also 1.000; wipeout already begins at dc=15 for rc=24 and dc=21 for rc=36 — an extinction line far wider than the pre-registered dc ≤6.
- P6 (the split band and split-type FPs): half. Splitting is real and its FP mean far exceeds the 0.3 line (2.75 across split cells), but the band is narrower and elsewhere than predicted: within the pre-registered rc=36 band (dc 6–32) only dc=12 splits at over half (0.667); dc=32 is 0 and dc=6 is already shattered past splitting. The cleanest splitting belongs to rc=24 at dc=6 (1.000) and dc=0 (0.833), outside the rc=36 hypothesis.

Two full hits, four halves. Both misses (P2 at rc=24, P6's band location) fail at the same place: I reasoned about this flower as a solid disc silhouette instead of by its actual construction. The petals are discrete ellipses; the occluder cuts the connections between them, and the harm morphology is set by the measured object's internal structure, not by the occluder's geometry.

## 4. Analysis and trade-offs

Putting the two clutter articles side by side, the full account compresses into one contrast. Same-colour clutter harms by merging: reclassification-type harm, a valley curve peaking at a light touch, lost always 0, FPs supplied by the clutter itself when separated. Heterochromatic occlusion harms by deletion: loss- and area-underestimation-type harm, broadly monotone, reclassification in the opposite direction, FPs supplied by host shards. Three judgements a deployment can take away:

First, ask for the occluder's colour before its geometry. An occluder outside the target colour ranges (a leaf, a shadow edge, another crop's foliage) is extinction-level for small targets: a bud whose solo area is only about three times the floor is gone entirely once its centre is covered, and it leaves no FP trace — totals only ever shrink, and nothing in the frame looks wrong. Recall validation for small targets must include occluded samples by construction; a recall of 1.000 measured on clean samples (this series' baseline) does not survive contact with a leafy field.

Second, area-fed downstreams must defend against systematic underestimation. In the biting regime (before any splitting), a bloom's area ratio slides to 0.52 (rc=36, dc=32) while its label and the count stay perfect — and any downstream that eats contour areas (this series' scale estimator eats area medians) will be fed a downward bias that trips no count alarm. Right counts, wrong areas is this harm type at its most insidious, and it is the same shape as the common-mode bias the marker-guard line measured: alarms must be set on area itself, not entrusted to counts firing first.

Third, split-type FPs are a bookkeeping problem, not a detector problem. One true bloom reporting 3.5 FPs is fragment accounting: merge same-neighbourhood shards before counting, or let the evaluation convention allow several fragments to jointly claim one truth, and most of that bill disappears; retuning detector thresholds would only cause collateral damage. This is the exact mirror of the bridge article's conclusion — merging squeezes several truths into one detection, splitting scatters one truth into several detections, and evaluation bookkeeping owes both directions a claiming path.

None of this costs deployment compute: all three are changes to validation-sample composition and evaluation bookkeeping, not to detector parameters.

## 5. Limitations and open questions

- The bloom area ratio carries real baseline noise: the separated control cells (disc 10 px clear of the flower, nominally zero effect) read 0.890/0.958/0.960 for rc=12/24/36, because every redrawn bloom re-samples its petal count and rotation and the solo median is only an expected reference. Every bloom area ratio in this article carries roughly ±0.05–0.10 of sampling noise; small rises and falls (such as 0.983 at the rc=12 tangent cell) should not be over-read. The bud is a single ellipse and far quieter (separated cells 1.002–1.010); its curves deserve more confidence.
- One occluder colour (foliage base) and one shape (solid disc). Translucent leaves, textured leaves, and shadows — soft-edged occluders — would leave a half-mask pixel band at the boundary and may land between the same-colour and heterochromatic morphologies; untested. The 2026-11-07 shape account suggests shape moves thresholds; the heterochromatic side has not swept shape.
- Host radii are fixed at representative values: the bud's r=15 is the top of its range, and an r=11 bud (solo area about 250) would have a wider extinction line still; untested. Small blooms (r=30) and their island morphology are likewise untested.
- Single host only. A green disc covering two adjacent flowers at once is untested — the mirror of the bridge problem (a heterochromatic bridge does not merge, it double-kills), left as a follow-up topic.
- All evidence is still this simulator: the discrete-petal construction is this simulator's drawing method, and real bloom petals overlap, so a concentric cover may not shatter a real flower into islands. What transfers is the structure — occlusion is extinction-level for small targets, systematically underestimates area, sources its FPs from shards, and makes centre-point ledgers overstate loss. The pixel-level numbers (extinction-line dc values, area ratios) must be re-measured on any other detector or on real photographs.
