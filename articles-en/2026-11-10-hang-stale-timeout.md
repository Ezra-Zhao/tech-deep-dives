# The Process Is Still Alive, but Every Scrape Burns the Full 4 Seconds: Staleness Under a Timeout Death, Measured

Author: Guangyi Zhao
Date: 2026-11-10
Subject: Scrape-timeout (hang) death vs SIGTERM death for Prometheus staleness and absent() blind windows, measured live on the author's net-assurance-lab bench (Prometheus 3.15.0, loopback only)

## 1. Problem and background

The earlier entries in this series measured absent() blind windows under one kind of death: SIGTERM. The exporter process exits, the port refuses connections instantly, and a failed scrape ends in milliseconds. The 2026-10-27 article measured a first-tier blind window of about 75 seconds; the 2026-10-31 article showed that series carrying their own timestamps live in a second tier of about 370 seconds, and that a recording-rule replica follows its source. But the death production systems actually die is usually not a clean exit. It is a process that is still alive, still accepting connections, and never answering: a deadlock, a stop-the-world pause that never ends, a thread pool quietly exhausted. Every scrape connects successfully and then waits the full scrape_timeout — 4 seconds in this bench's configuration — before failing. The topic pool charged this article with one specific bill: run the same three accounting paths (plain scrape, own timestamps, recording rule) under this slow-failure death, measure when the staleness marker lands and what the absent blind window becomes, and compare against the SIGTERM refusal death.

Intuition offers two opposite answers. One says staleness binds to the scrape-failure event itself — a failure is a failure, and a slow one costs at most one timeout plus alignment, leaving every tier where it was. The other says a refused connection means the target is unambiguously gone, while a timeout merely means "not yet" — if Prometheus treats the two differently, a hung target's series might escape the staleness marker entirely, survive the full 5-minute lookback, and push the first-tier blind window up to the second tier's magnitude. Only a live run settles it.

The headline: every path stayed in its SIGTERM tier. Plain scrape fired in 67.1–70.1 seconds (SIGTERM: 74.2–75.5). Own-timestamp fired in 368.6–371.1 seconds (SIGTERM: 369.4–373.8). The recorded replica stayed in lockstep with its source (both pending at 19.9 seconds, activeAt identical to the microsecond). The slow failure left exactly one systematic trace, one level down: series vanished from instant queries one poll later than under SIGTERM — 10.0 seconds instead of about 5 (the recorded replica at 15.1 seconds) — roughly the price of waiting for the first failed scrape to finish. At the alert layer, the 15-second evaluation alignment noise swallowed even that: firing deltas against SIGTERM were −7.1, −0.8, and +5.8 seconds across the three paths (by lower bound), not even consistent in sign. Staleness binds to the failure event. How slow the failure is does not change its tier.

## 2. Method

The configuration matches the 2026-10-31 article word for word, deliberately, so the numbers stay comparable across articles: Prometheus 3.15.0 (official tarball, 111,651,578 bytes), a fresh instance and TSDB per path; scrape interval 5 seconds, scrape timeout 4 seconds, rule-group evaluation interval 15 seconds, all three absent alerts with for: 1m and the same expressions as the earlier articles. The design and four preregistered predictions were frozen to disk before the run (data/2026-11-10-hang-stale-prereg.md).

- Path C, plain scrape: the lab's real exporter (src/prom_exporter.py) serving a copy of run 20261019-171627's artifacts; samples carry no timestamps of their own.
- Path T, own timestamps: the minimal exporter from the 2026-10-31 article, every sample stamped with an explicit millisecond timestamp taken at request time; the only deliberate difference from C is the timestamp.
- Path R, recording rule: same scrape as C, plus a recording rule replicating the present series as nal_present_recorded, with two absent alerts running side by side in the same death.

The death itself is the design point that needs spelling out. Instead of killing the exporter, I froze it with SIGSTOP. The process does not exit (its PID stays alive), its listening socket remains in the kernel, TCP handshakes still complete in the kernel — but the process never reads and never replies. To a scraper, that is observably identical to a real hang: connect succeeds, the full timeout burns, zero bytes arrive. Immediately after each freeze, an out-of-band probe (not via Prometheus) certified the death shape: read the /proc state, open a raw TCP connection, and issue an HTTP GET with a 5-second probe timeout. All three probes passed: process state T (stopped), TCP connect in about 0.0 seconds, and zero bytes received within 5.005–5.008 seconds.

A second self-certification comes from Prometheus itself: scrape_duration_seconds after the freeze. On path C, all 18 poll readings fell between 4.0005 and 4.0153 seconds — every scrape ran the full 4-second timeout, none failed fast. On paths T and R, the first post-freeze poll can still show the last healthy scrape's duration (0.0018 and 0.0823 seconds — an instant query returns the most recently completed scrape), and every reading after that is about 4.00 seconds (one 4.0562 on path R). The flow matches the earlier articles: hold a healthy baseline for 30 seconds, freeze, probe, poll until the main alert fires, hold 20 more seconds, then SIGCONT and clean up. Blind-window measurement is unchanged: pending from the alert API's activeAt, firing as an interval (activeAt + for:, first poll that observes firing), 5-second poll granularity, no finer precision claimed. promtool check rules returned SUCCESS on both rule files (1 and 3 rules).

One execution detail, reported plainly: all three phases reproduced the cold-start race seen before — during the first 4 baseline polls the absent alert was already pending (the first series had not been scraped yet), it cleared at the next evaluation once the series appeared, never fired, and the baseline was clean before the freeze. The post-freeze pending carries a second, distinct activeAt and is not confused with the startup one.

## 3. Measurements

### 3.1 Path C: same tier, series vanish one poll later

At the first 5-second poll after the freeze, present was still there — the first scrape was still burning its 4 seconds. At the +10.0-second poll, present was empty in instant queries, up flipped to 0 in the same poll, and absent() returned 1 in the same poll. The absent alert went pending 7.1 seconds after the freeze (the first evaluation cycle after the staleness marker landed; the evaluation clock is independent of my 5-second polling, so its activeAt can precede the poll that first observes the series gone), and firing fell in 67.1–70.1 seconds.

Against SIGTERM, item by item: present empty at 10.0 vs 5.1 seconds (4.9 later, about one poll); pending 7.1 vs 14.2 (7.1 earlier); firing lower bound 67.1 vs 74.2 (7.1 earlier). Pending and firing were not merely undelayed by the timeout — they landed a full step earlier than the instant-refusal run. That is not the slow death being faster; it is the phase noise of a 15-second evaluation clock. Which evaluation cycle pending lands in depends on the relative phase of the freeze and the evaluation ticks, and ±7 seconds between single runs is well inside that noise band (path R produced +5.8 seconds in the opposite direction under the same mechanism). The defensible claim is magnitude: the two deaths' firing times differ by less than one evaluation interval, and the up-to-9-seconds cost preregistration had budgeted for the slow failure is not measurable at the alert layer.

The verdict on preregistered P-C, recorded honestly, is "half": the direction and tier judgment held (present empty inside the preregistered ≤10.5-second bound, blind window in the first tier), but two numeric bands missed literally — pending at 7.1 seconds fell 1.9 seconds below the 9–25-second band, and the firing lower bound at 67.1 seconds fell 1.9 seconds below the 69–90-second band. The error was not in the mechanism judgment; it was in centering the bands on a single SIGTERM run whose phase I treated as a midpoint. Phase is a noise source, not a baseline. Section 4 turns this into a rule.

### 3.2 Path T: the full lookback, to the second

After the freeze, up flipped to 0 at the +10.0-second poll (waiting for the first scrape to burn its timeout, same as C). present did not follow. It stayed visible in instant queries until the +300.7-second poll first showed it empty, with absent() returning 1 in that same poll. Converted to the samples' own timeline: the last visible sample before the freeze carries a timestamp 5.01 seconds before the freeze moment, and whether one more successful scrape landed inside the final 5 seconds cannot be resolved at this polling granularity, so survival is an interval — the series lived 300.7–305.7 seconds past its last sample's timestamp, i.e. the 300-second lookback plus alignment, identical to the SIGTERM run (303.1–308.1 seconds).

The alert stayed in the second tier: pending at 308.6 seconds, firing in 368.6–371.1 seconds. Deltas against SIGTERM are −0.8 seconds (pending and firing lower bound) and −2.7 seconds (firing poll against the earlier interval's upper bound). In other words, an own-timestamp series does not take a staleness marker from a scrape that rotted in a timeout either — a failed scrape, fast or slow, writes nothing to it, and it lives out the full lookback before disappearing. Preregistered P-T hit in full (present about 300 seconds, pending band 300–330, firing band 360–395).

### 3.3 Path R: replica in lockstep; the extra poll shows only in queries

After the freeze, present emptied at the +10.0-second poll, same as C. recorded held one poll longer, first empty at +15.1 seconds — the recording rule needs one more evaluation (inside its 15-second interval) to see an empty source before the replica takes its own staleness marker, and that step is visible at the query layer. At the alert layer, the two absent alerts were again perfectly synchronized: activeAt identical to the microsecond (both pending 19.9 seconds after the freeze), firing identical at 79.9–80.3 seconds. The replica's 5-second later disappearance at the query layer propagated to no alert timing at all — at the for: 1m scale, a 5-second head start is absorbed completely. Against SIGTERM: pending and firing lower bound both +5.8 seconds, firing poll +5.1, present empty +5.0 — noise in the other direction. Preregistered P-R hit in full (same-cycle pending difference of 0, main alert firing inside the 69–105-second band).

### 3.4 The up ledger

Preregistered P-UP also hit in full, and uniformly across paths in a way that looks rehearsed: the first up=0 poll was +10.0 seconds after the freeze on all three paths, against +5.0/+5.1 seconds under SIGTERM. The price the slow failure charges for "the scrape layer knows the target is dead" is exactly one timeout plus alignment — about 5 seconds, no more, no less. Set beside absent, the shape of the conclusion is clean. On a first-tier path, up flips at 10 seconds and absent fires at 67–80 seconds; the bulk of that gap is for: plus evaluation alignment, not the death. On the second-tier (own-timestamp) path, the gap is still about 300 seconds, and the death is again absent from it. When budgeting any chain's blind window, the shape of the death — process gone versus process hung — is not a variable worth modeling. The accounting method (the two tiers of the 2026-10-31 article) is.

## 4. Analysis and trade-offs

First, the portable conclusion: staleness binds to the scrape-failure event, not to the failure's shape. Under this configuration, connection-refused and the 4-second timeout produced same-tier absent blind windows on all three paths, with deltas inside ±7 seconds, inconsistent in sign, and smaller than the 15-second rule-group evaluation interval. Changing scrape_timeout to any other value will not move a chain's blind window from one tier to another. Conversely, if absent is late to fire in production, "the target died slowly" is the wrong place to look; the two-tier table from the 2026-10-31 article — who provides the sample timestamps — is the right one. Evidence grade A within this bench's configuration: three paths, one live run each, the death shape certified twice over (out-of-band probe and scrape durations).

Second, where the slow failure's cost actually lives: the series layer, not the alert layer. present and recorded emptied about 5 seconds later (the replica 5 more), and up flipped about 5 seconds later — the direct footprint of waiting for the first failed scrape to finish, on the order of one scrape_timeout plus alignment. Systems that act on instant queries (using query results to drain traffic, for example) should budget those 5 seconds. Systems that act on absent alerts will never see the charge; for: buries it.

Third, a methodological correction to my own preregistration practice, recorded as it happened. P-C's numeric bands missed because I centered them on a single SIGTERM measurement, while the pending/firing quantities contain an evaluation-phase component with noise on the order of ± one evaluation interval. A single run does not earn the right to be a band center. The corrected rule for later rounds of this series: any preregistered band for a quantity containing an evaluation-alignment component must be at least ±1 evaluation interval wide, and the baseline must be labeled a single phase sample. This run's three deltas against SIGTERM (−7.1, −0.8, +5.8 seconds) are themselves the first first-hand sample of that noise band: all three inside ±7.1 seconds, consistent with half an evaluation interval.

Fourth, a division-of-labor note for exporter authors and monitoring designers, in line with the earlier articles: do not defend against the death; classify the accounting. A chain whose only health signal is absent reacts to a hang exactly as it reacts to a kill — roughly 75 seconds in tier one, roughly 370 in tier two — set by accounting, not by the death. The signals that are sensitive to a hang are the scrape-layer ones, up and scrape duration: their delay here was on the order of 10 seconds, and the duration itself (a steady ~4.00 seconds against milliseconds when healthy) is the earliest fingerprint separating "hung" from "dead". If a target still connects but every scrape burns the full timeout, look inside the process (deadlock, long GC, thread pool) before looking at the network or at port liveness.

Nothing in the lab repository was modified in this run. The freeze acted only on exporter processes this run started itself, and everything was SIGCONT-ed and cleaned up.

## 5. Limitations and open questions

- Only one hang shape was measured: a full SIGSTOP freeze. Real hangs have livelier variants — slow responses whose scrape times straddle the timeout, succeeding intermittently, would reset staleness and lookback between successes; that blind-window shape is outside this run's configuration and is the next measurable variable on this axis.
- SIGSTOP freezes the whole process. A single deadlocked handler thread while other endpoints stay alive, or a healthy exporter whose downstream data source is stuck (the exporter answers quickly with old values or errors), are different deaths; the latter is not even a scrape failure and is not covered by any conclusion here.
- Each path ran once. The cross-death deltas (about ±7 seconds) are the same size as evaluation-phase noise, so this run supports only the bound "difference smaller than one evaluation interval", not a finer estimate. Pinning the delta below the second would take repeated runs per death to accumulate a phase distribution.
- On path R, recorded emptying one poll (5 seconds) after present is bounded by the 5-second polling granularity, as in the 2026-10-31 article; the step-by-step mechanism (source staleness vs recording staleness ordering) is recorded as the explanation consistent with the data, not resolved more finely.
- scrape_timeout of 4 seconds against a 5-second interval is this bench's configuration. When the timeout approaches or exceeds the interval, scrapes start overlapping and being skipped; staleness timing in that regime is unmeasured, and the conclusions here are limited to timeout-below-interval configurations.
- Loopback, a single chain, and for: 1m bound the numbers as in the earlier articles; absolute figures like 370 seconds are tied to the default 300-second lookback, and chains that change lookback-delta should recompute from the two-tier framework rather than copy numbers.
- Open questions: the exporter dying behind a still-living remote-write sender, and the intermittent-success half-death, remain queued in the topic pool. After this article, the death-shape axis has exactly one unmeasured cell left: intermittent success.
